How Block0 works

Updated 1 October 2026.

Block0 reads Robinhood Chain launches from their own transfer history on the public node and reports what happened: who bought first, who bought together, who holds the supply, who is selling, and what the deployer launched before. Every number is reproducible from public data. It does not predict price — and below is the record of the tests that tried to, and failed.

What Block0 reads

  • Early wallets — snipers (first buy within 3 blocks of the first pool trade) and bundles (several of them in the same block). This is a timing fact. It is consistent with insiders, but it does not prove who controls a wallet, so we say "early wallets", not "insiders".
  • Selling means tokens sent into a trading venue — the token's pool or bonding curve, the chain's AMM, a known router. Tokens sent to another wallet or a contract are reported separately as transferred out: moving a bag is not a sale.
  • "Now" means the 30 minutes before the read, timed from the chain head — not the token's last trade. A quiet token has nothing "now". Every board row and dossier shows when it was read; a row read more than 45 minutes ago no longer shows the "selling now" fields at all.
  • Concentration — the share of the distributed supply held by the ten largest wallets (the curve, pools and routers excluded).
  • Deployer / owner — what else the same wallet launched. For an Orbio agent, checked against every Orbio agent; for a Pons launch, against the launchpad's latest 100 launches and every graduated token.
  • Smart-money radar — 738 wallets with closed, profitable round trips on two or more launches (contracts excluded), read live in your browser. A buy needs evidence of a trade (a known venue, or payment in the same transaction) and must be sent by the wallet itself; a wallet that sells or moves the tokens out stops counting. The radar sees only its window, so it says "bought and has not sold or moved it since", never "holds". The list is built from launches up to 2026-09-09 and ages.

Where it reads from: the free public Robinhood Chain node, the Pons launchpad's own catalogue, the Orbio protocol API, and DexScreener / GeckoTerminal for price context. The Block0 agent re-reads every Pons and Orbio launch under 72 hours old (≥$5k, ≥5 holders) about every 15 minutes.

What we tested — and what failed

Every claim that Block0's reads could predict something was tested against history with the rule written before the result. None passed.

So the reads on this site are descriptive: they tell you what a launch's holders did. They are not shown to tell you what its price will do.

The one open test

Whether proven wallets converging on a launch means anything is being measured forward, in public, with the rules fixed before the first event (tools/radar-protocol.mjs). Every convergence is logged when it is seen, priced at that moment, next to up to four launches of similar size and age that no tracked wallet touched. Seven days later each is scored from market candles that start after detection, against its own controls only. No verdict before 30 scored events; the running report is public.

Protocol v3 (2026-10-01 follow-up) excludes any buyer with an observed sale, transfer out or deposit from convergence, including partial outflows. A verdict also needs at least 30 paired seven-day closing outcomes and 80% closing coverage in each chronological half (the early 70% / late 30% split); each paired event needs 80% of its own logged controls covered. Insufficient coverage means no verdict, not a pass or fail. These are completeness safeguards, not proof of statistical power. Earlier v1/v2 events stay in the log, counted and not scored under v3. Until the report says PASS, the radar is unvalidated — and the agent does not post convergence at all.

The risk verdict

Cards and dossiers carry a 0–100 risk score: a summary of the facts above, not a forecast — the tests above found it does not predict outcomes. It is a transparent weighted composite of five sub-scores, each computed from the token's own transfer history over its real distributed float (the bonding curve and known venues are excluded so their reserves don't read as a giant holder).

*Weights shift once a token graduates: concentration and live dumping matter more for an established coin, launch-mechanics (bundles) matter less. On top of the weighted mean sit three disqualifying floors — near-total concentration (top-10 ≥ 82–92%), two-plus insiders selling right now, or a coordinated cluster that owns a real slice AND is distributing this minute. Any one forces a high score regardless of everything else, because it single-handedly defines the risk and must not be offset by a clean sub-score elsewhere.

Everything in this section describes research that is parked. It is kept because it is the basis for the failed tests above, not because it is in use: nothing on the board or in the agent's posts uses the resemblance score, the corridor or the ladder.

Resemblance to past winners

These are two different questions, and we keep them apart. The risk score above is a fact we can check now — how manipulated the launch looks. This second read is a historical resemblance score (0–100): how closely a fresh launch matches the shape the biggest past winners had at the same age. It guides attention — it is not a probability of success. A launch can look clean and still fail; a messy one can still run. Validated discriminators, in order of weight:

  • No bundles (up to 40 pts) — the hard veto; coordinated launches rarely become winners.
  • A float that opens up (up to 25 pts) — top-10 concentration falling below 40–60%.
  • A settled, clean score (up to 20 pts) — risk under 25–45.
  • Real adoption (up to 15 pts) — 80–200+ actual holders.

It deliberately does not reward low sniper activity — the winner cohort showed heavy early snipers were survivable, while bundles were what killed. A strong resemblance is worth a closer look; it is a pattern, not a promise. The comparison set is every launch that closed above $1M for a day — the full class, including the ones that later faded, not a handful of survivors — but this read is still guidance to focus your attention, never odds; it strengthens as the cohort grows (see the measured limits below).

The winner corridor & valuation ladder

These come from a study of a selected catalog cohort, labelled by observed outcomes — the pipeline supports Pons and direct DEX listings, but actual venue coverage depends on the committed cohort. Graduation is not a criterion. The study asks two separate questions, because they have very different amounts of evidence behind them:

  • Q1 — will it reach $1M? A token "reached" if it closed above $1M for a full day (its held-peak, so a one-swap wick doesn't count). That is a past fact: once it happens it can never be undone or censored, so every token that did it counts — the ones that held, the ones that later faded, and the ones still too young to judge. The corridor and the valuation ladder are fitted on this class.
  • Q2 — will it hold? Among the tokens that reached $1M: did it stay above $1M for a week or more with its holders intact (a sustainable path, not a spike), or collapse? Fewer tokens, a harder question — reported on its own, never folded into Q1.

Each reached token's whole history is replayed and reduced to a trajectory (distribution health blended with real wallet inflow) and a wallets→market-cap curve.

"Held-peak" is the highest market cap the token stayed at for a full day (reconstructed caps wick on single swaps). Pending and mid tokens are excluded from both sides — a three-day-old token above $1M can't have held it a week yet, so it is never counted as a loser (right-censoring). The launchpad's own token and quote assets are excluded.

  • The corridor is the winners' healthy trajectory zone (25th–75th percentile) at each age. A live token's own trajectory is drawn through it — inside the band is tracking the winners, below is drifting.
  • Stage targets — each corridor box also shows the median unique-wallet count and market cap the winners actually carried by that age, so you see whether a token is hitting the real adoption and valuation a main player needs at each stage.
  • The valuation ladder places a token's current wallet count against what winners were worth at the same count.

The model is a committed, reproducible pipeline — every backtest is cached and re-labelled with live market caps, so the cohort compounds run after run and the model refreshes as pending tokens settle into winners or fades.

One venue, so far. Every winner in the model to date launched on the Pons launchpad. A healthy first hour on a bonding-curve launch is not the same shape as a token listed straight on the DEX, so a direct-DEX lookup is scored against Pons winners and marked lower-confidence (the read says "cross-venue"). When enough DEX launches reach and hold a real cap, they get their own calibration; until then we disclose the gap rather than pretend the shapes are identical.

Does it actually work?

The honest question — for Q1, "will it reach $1M?". Below is a separation test: at each age, how well the trajectory tells the tokens that went on to close above $1M apart from the ones that never did. Two controls are graded: all (stalled + dead + faded below the bar) and faded only — tokens that got to $300k and then collapsed without ever holding $1M, the ones that look most like runners early. AUC is the probability a random reacher scores above a random control: 0.50 is a coin flip, 1.00 is perfect. Q2 (will it hold) is measured separately further down.

Read this honestly. The per-age table is in-sample separation on a small cohort (the chain is young). Winners are graded leave-one-out and the controls never shaped the band, and the time split below fits the band on earlier launches and grades only the later ones — the closest thing to out-of-sample a two-month-old chain allows. Early samples are sparse and weak; the table does not establish that waiting an hour produces a useful entry signal. Treat a very fresh launch's read as low-confidence by design, and the whole model as evidence that compounds as more launches settle, not a guarantee.

Limits — what this can't tell you

  • Reads have an age. The board is the agent's latest read of each launch, up to a few hours old; each row says when it was read.
  • Some sales are invisible as sales. Selling into a bonding curve pays native ETH, which leaves no log. The radar can tell it is a sale only when a tracked wallet bought from the same curve in its window; otherwise it shows as tokens moved into a contract.
  • Prices are market context. Market caps come from the launchpad and DexScreener; candles from GeckoTerminal, which only has hours with trades. None of them is part of a read.
  • On-chain can't see intent. A clean distribution can still be a slow rug; a messy one can still run. This reads structure, not the future.
  • The first half hour is noise. A launch minutes old has too few transfers to read; cards label it "too early".
